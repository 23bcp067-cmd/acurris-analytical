QuEChERS (quick, easy, cheap, effective, rugged and safe) is the standard extraction for multi-residue pesticide analysis, and labs now use it for veterinary drugs and other contaminants too. Every version follows the same two steps. You shake the sample with acetonitrile and a salt mix so the analytes move into the solvent layer. Then you clean a portion of that extract with dispersive SPE (dSPE) sorbents before injection.

Two choices decide most of your results: which salt formulation you extract with, and which sorbents you clean up with.

## The three salt formulations

| Formulation | Salts per tube | Sample | Buffer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Original | 4 g MgSO4, 1 g NaCl | 10 g | None |
| AOAC 2007.01 | 6 g MgSO4, 1.5 g sodium acetate | 15 g | Acetate (with 1% acetic acid in acetonitrile) |
| EN 15662 | 4 g MgSO4, 1 g NaCl, 1 g trisodium citrate dihydrate, 0.5 g disodium hydrogen citrate sesquihydrate | 10 g | Citrate |

Magnesium sulfate pulls water out of the acetonitrile and drives the phase split. Sodium chloride controls how polar the solvent layer is. The buffers hold the pH steady, which protects pesticides that break down or ionize at the wrong pH.

## How to choose

**Follow your accreditation.** If your NABL scope lists AOAC 2007.01 or EN 15662, use that formulation. Changing salts changes recoveries for some analytes, and an assessor will expect revalidation.

**For a new method, start buffered.** Both buffered versions give steadier recoveries for pH-sensitive compounds than the original. Labs that also report to European buyers often pick EN 15662 so their method matches the buyer's reference.

**Match sample weight to the tube.** The salt weights assume a fixed sample mass. If you scale the sample down, scale the salts with it.

## Dry samples need water

Cereals, pulses, spices, tea and dried fruit hold too little water for the salts to partition against. Labs reduce the sample weight and add water before extraction, typically bringing the mix to around 80 percent water. Let dry samples soak for a set time after adding water and keep that time the same from batch to batch.

## Picking dSPE sorbents

Raw QuEChERS extracts carry sugars, fats, organic acids and pigments. These foul GC liners, build up on LC columns and suppress signal in mass spectrometry. Pick the sorbent by what your matrix carries.

| Sorbent | Removes | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| PSA (primary secondary amine) | Sugars, fatty acids, organic acids | Almost every fruit and vegetable |
| C18 | Lipids and other nonpolar co-extractives | Oilseeds, nuts, milk, meat, fish |
| GCB (graphitized carbon black) | Chlorophyll, carotenoids, sterols | Spinach, leafy greens, tea, chili |
| MgSO4 | Residual water | Added to every blend |

GCB has one catch. It also retains planar pesticides such as thiabendazole and hexachlorobenzene. If those are on your list, use the smallest GCB load that clears the color, and check recoveries with spiked samples.

## Common Indian matrices

| Matrix | Extraction notes | Typical cleanup |
|---|---|---|
| Rice, wheat, pulses | Add water, soak before extraction | PSA + MgSO4, add C18 for bran-rich samples |
| Chili, turmeric, spices | Small sample, add water | PSA + C18 + GCB, check pigmented-matrix recoveries |
| Tea | Small sample, add water | PSA + GCB + MgSO4 |
| Groundnut, oilseeds | High fat, freeze-out step helps | PSA + C18 + MgSO4 |
| Fruits, vegetables | No water needed | PSA + MgSO4, add GCB for dark greens |

Treat these as starting points. Validate recoveries for your own analyte list on each matrix before you report results.

## Before you switch suppliers

Ask for the exact fill weights and salt grades, and compare them line by line with your method. Then run a recovery check on spiked blanks of your most difficult matrix. An evaluation pack is enough for one recovery study.
