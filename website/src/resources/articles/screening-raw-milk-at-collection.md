Raw milk reaches a dairy through many small hands, and one contaminated can spoils a pooled tanker. Testing at the collection point, before milk is pooled, is the cheapest place to catch problems. This guide covers the three groups most dairies screen for, and the handling steps that decide whether rapid tests give reliable answers.

## What to screen for

| Group | What it catches | Typical test | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Antibiotic residues | Milk from animals under treatment, sold before the withdrawal period ends | Receptor or lateral flow test, often combined beta-lactam and tetracycline | Collection center, plant intake |
| Adulterants | Urea, starch, detergent, neutralizers, synthetic milk | Color-change spot tests | Collection center |
| Aflatoxin M1 | Carryover from moldy feed eaten by the animal | Lateral flow or ELISA | Plant intake, chilling center |

FSSAI sets limits for antibiotic residues and aflatoxin M1 in milk, and export buyers may apply stricter ones. Check the current Food Safety and Standards (Contaminants, Toxins and Residues) Regulations and your buyers' specifications, then choose kits whose detection levels sit at or below the strictest limit you face.

## Sample handling decides the result

**Mix before you sample.** Fat rises quickly. Stir or plunge the can or tank before you draw milk, then take the sample from the middle.

**Keep it cold.** Test within the window your kit allows, and refrigerate samples you can't test at once. Warm milk grows bacteria, and some of them interfere with receptor tests.

**Don't test colostrum or milk from mastitic animals as normal milk.** Both can give false positives on antibiotic tests.

## Running the tests

**Calibrate the incubator.** Antibiotic tests depend on a fixed incubation temperature. Check your dry block with a thermometer every week. A block running two degrees off changes results.

**Read on time.** Lateral flow lines keep developing after the read window. A strip read at fifteen minutes when the kit says eight can show a false negative or a false positive. Use a timer for every test.

**Use one light source.** Read color-change adulterant tests under the same light every time, against the reference card. Morning daylight and evening tube light make the same color look different.

**Run a control.** Run a known negative milk sample once per shift, and a positive control when you open a new kit lot.

## Building a panel

Adulterated milk rarely carries only one additive, because each additive hides a different quality check. Urea raises apparent protein, starch raises solids, and detergent emulsifies added oil. A panel at intake catches what any single test misses. Most dairies start with:

- Combined beta-lactam and tetracycline test on every collection
- Urea, starch, detergent and synthetic milk on every collection or on a rotating schedule
- Aflatoxin M1 at plant intake or chilling center, on pooled milk

Add tests based on what local veterinary practice and past results tell you.

## When a test is positive

Treat every rapid positive as presumptive.

1. Hold the milk. Don't pool it.
2. Retest the same sample with a fresh test.
3. If it is still positive, send a portion to a lab for confirmation. Confirmatory labs use LC-MS/MS for antibiotic residues and HPLC or LC-MS/MS for aflatoxin M1.
4. Record the supplier, date, quantity and result.

Records turn a single rejection into a pattern you can raise with the supplier or the village society. They also show regulators and buyers that your screening program works.

## Training collection staff

Most collection-center tests need no lab skills, but they do need consistency. Train staff on the four steps that cause most errors: mixing, sample temperature, incubation and read time. A laminated one-page procedure at each testing point helps more than a long manual.
